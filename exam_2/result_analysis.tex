\subsubsection{LMS}
Starting with the output of the regular LMS filter, it can be seen on figure:\ref{fig:comp_LMS_M3}, 
where the size of the $\mu$ is very small and how it compares to an increase in size in an order og magitude
twice. Towards the end of the 2000 samples, $y[k]$ seems to almost be equal to that of $d[k]$ meaning that
the error, which is the difference of two would become zero, this would essentailly mean that the filter
that converged on the weights of the unidentified system. However as the value of $\mu$ increases, the speed 
at which this happens increases, quite dramatically.\\

Taking a look at the graph for the weights, the semi dashed lines, belonging to $\mu = 0.005$, the weights have almost converged after 500 samples, which is a fourth of 
the total amount of samples. And again, an increases in this speed, or decrease in time, can be see if the
value is even larger. Looking at the equation for the update of the weights, $\mu$ is 
essentailly just scaling how much we want the product of the error and the old samples,
to affect the future weights. As this goes on for every iteration, it becomes intuitive how it
slows the entire process down.
\\

We can consider an example where we let every input sample be $x[k] = 1$ and we have only the first term in our
unidentified system with the difference equation $d[k]= 0.5x[k]$, and the filter is initialized to be $w[0] = 0$. We want 

to find the $w_{ideal} = 0.5$ such that the error is forced to 0.
A entire iteration with $\mu=0.05$ of the LMS becomes:
\[d[k] = 0.5(1) = 0.5\]
\[y[0] = w[k]x[k] = 0(1) = 0\]
\[e[k] = 0.5 - 0 = 0.5\]
\[w[k+1] = w[k] + 2\mu e(k)x(k) = 0 + 2(0.05)(0.5)(1) = 0.05 \]

\subsection{Discussion}
As alluded to in the LMS analysis section, larger values of $\mu$ is not necessary a good thing.
If too a large is selected, the resulting product of the input and the error is essentailly gained
by the value, if this value $\mu \le 1$ it is easy to see that, this will cause the resulting calculations
to start spiralling out of control. An optimum value for $\mu$ can be derived, however as long as:
\[0 < \mu < \frac{1}{\lambda_{max}}\]
This is equation (6.17) from the adaptive filter book,\cite[p.143]{ASPbook}
"where the $\lambda_{max}$ is the maximum eigenvalue of $\mathbf{R}$"\cite[p.143]{ASPbook}, where $\mathbf{R}$ is the autocorrelation matrix.\\
To observe how well the regular LMS performs in our experiments, we've taken a look at much $y[k]$ approches $d[k]$ which would
result in the error converging to zero. This can be represented as the mean square error instead, and when
this is done over multiple runs of the filter\cite{wiki_mse}.
\[MSE = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^2_i[k]\]
Letting this complete a 1000 runs, we produce a new expression for the estimate of the expected 
squared error at each sample interval. This results in the following graph:
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{graphs/LMS_mse.png}
    \caption{The mean squared error of the LMS, with varying sizes of $\mu$}
    \label{fig:LMS_EMS}
\end{figure}


On figure \ref{fig:LMS_EMS} it is possible to see the decreasing nature of the error,
it seems smooth, however this averages over 1000 runs, this does not mean that the filter
itself will be smooth, but over the averaged runs, it is. The reason for this type of result 
is also the choice of input, where white gaussian noise is exhibits some fluctuations, when averaged
over that many runs, it will average out smoothly. The curve also shows the filters
ability, in this case to converge towards 0, meaning the filter has approximated the unidentified system.\\

%discuss why the adaptation of step size, continues even after the weights have converged
An interesting observation can made when comparing figure:\ref{fig:VVSLMS_1_adapt} and
\ref{fig:VVSLMS_1_conv}. It can be seen that the weights of the filter have converged 
relatively fast, however the step-size adaptation continues to grow, long after the weights.
This is a phenomenon unique to the VSS-LMS. Taking a look at the equations for updating the
weights $w_i[k+1]$: \ref{VSS_LMS_W} and 
the $\mu$ update equation \ref{VSS_LMS_mu_update} from earlier. This particular implementation
contains the function $sign(g_i[k])$ and $sign(g_i[k-1])$ this is used to estimate the gradient
of the error, resulting in
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\mu_i[k] = 
    \begin{cases}
        +1 & \text{$g_i[k]$ and $g_i[k-1] \qquad same \ sign$} \\
        -1 & \text{$g_i[k]$ and $g_i[k-1] \qquad opposite \ sign$} \\
        0 & \text{Either gradient is zero}
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:gradient_signs}
\end{equation}
The previous sample from $g_i[k]$, then could be a very small number, if an assumption is made that
the filter weights have converged. Then only very small changes will make it into the $g_i[k]$ equation, 
but since the $\mu$ update only cares about the sign, this will either add or subtract $\rho$ from $\mu$,
however in the weight update equation, $\rho$ is just a factor with $e[k]x[k-1]$, which are very small numbers
, thus the weight update will become even smaller. And this process repeats for every iteration. This leads to a situation
where a adapted filter, will produce very small errors, but can accidentally start to integrate a large value of $\mu$, 
much like a wind-up mechanism in control systems. However this is mainly due to the fact that the 
input of the algorithm is being fed with gaussian noise.\\

%discuss the weight shapes in VVS LMS vs regular LMS
The shape of the weight curves are also interesting to look at, the shape of any of the normal LMS
weight curves on figures: \ref{fig:LMS_M_3_W}, \ref{fig:LMS_M_5_W} and \ref{fig:LMS_M_10_W} compared
to \ref{fig:VVSLMS_1_conv}, here it can be seen that regular LMS implementation has an almost
exponential decay when converging, for this type of input signal, this also intuitively makes sense
since $\mu$ is fixed, the weights increases with the magnitude of the error, so that large errors
leads to large filter changes, and vice versa. 
In terms of the variable step-size implementation, as already discussed, the running filter actively
changes the step size, so the factor $\mu$ can increase, but the error introduced by $g[k]$ generally 
decreases as the filter slowly converges and they gradually cancel each other out.\\

%discuess the difficulties of actual implementation on slower systems
%resource usage, speed and bandwidth
On a practical note, implementing these algorithms on cheap hardware such as microcontrollers
might be an attractive prospective. Since the algorithms are only multiplication and additions,
with some sneaky pointer arithmatic, it should be fairly resource inexpensive. The limitations 
might come from actual floating point performance, as the weights and especially the step factors 
can become very small, thus it might introduce some interesting settling behaviour due to rounding errors.
